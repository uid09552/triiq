
# 80/20 Triathlon Training: TSS, CTL, ATL, Lactate, Threshold and Recovery

## Abstract

Triathlon training requires simultaneous management of swimming, cycling and running load. The athlete must accumulate sufficient training stress to create adaptation while maintaining enough recovery to avoid excessive fatigue, injury and performance decline.

The **80/20 training model** provides a practical framework in which approximately 80% of training is performed at low intensity and approximately 20% at moderate-to-high intensity. The exact distribution should be individualized according to training history, race distance, training phase and recovery capacity.

This whitepaper combines 80/20 intensity distribution with:

* Training Stress Score (TSS)
* Chronic Training Load (CTL)
* Acute Training Load (ATL)
* Training Stress Balance (TSB)
* Intensity Factor (IF)
* Functional Threshold Power (FTP)
* Lactate thresholds
* Heart-rate zones
* Running pace
* Swimming threshold pace
* Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE)
* Heart-rate variability (HRV)
* Resting heart rate
* Aerobic decoupling
* Energy availability
* Recovery and tapering

The fundamental principle is:

> **Training creates the stimulus. Recovery creates the adaptation.**

---

# 1. The 80/20 Principle

The 80/20 concept describes the distribution of training intensity.

A practical range is:

| Intensity               | Approx. training time | Purpose                                   |
| ----------------------- | --------------------: | ----------------------------------------- |
| Low intensity           |                75–85% | Aerobic development, durability, recovery |
| Moderate/high intensity |                15–25% | Threshold, VO₂max, race-specific work     |

The objective is not mathematical perfection.

The important distinction is:

> **Easy training should be genuinely easy, while hard training should have a clear purpose.**

A common problem in endurance athletes is excessive training in the middle-intensity range.

This creates:

* more fatigue than easy training
* less stimulus than properly executed hard training
* reduced recovery
* lower quality of subsequent sessions

---

# 2. Three-Zone Intensity Model

A simple physiological model divides training into three domains.

## Zone 1 — Low intensity

Generally below the first lactate or ventilatory threshold.

Typical characteristics:

* conversational breathing
* low lactate accumulation
* predominantly aerobic metabolism
* relatively low glycogen cost
* sustainable for long durations
* relatively rapid recovery

Typical RPE:

**2–3/10**

---

## Zone 2 — Moderate / Threshold Domain

Between the first and second physiological thresholds.

Characteristics:

* increasing carbohydrate utilization
* increasing lactate production
* increasing ventilation
* controlled but difficult breathing
* considerably higher recovery cost

Typical RPE:

**4–6/10**

This zone is useful, but excessive exposure can compromise recovery.

---

## Zone 3 — High Intensity

Above the second threshold.

Characteristics:

* high metabolic stress
* rapid fatigue
* high carbohydrate utilization
* high cardiovascular demand
* limited sustainable duration

Typical RPE:

**7–10/10**

Examples:

* VO₂max intervals
* short hill repetitions
* maximal swimming intervals
* short race-specific efforts
* anaerobic intervals

---

# 3. Threshold Physiology

Training zones should ideally be based on individual physiological thresholds.

Important concepts include:

* LT1 — first lactate threshold
* LT2 — second lactate threshold
* VT1 — first ventilatory threshold
* VT2 — second ventilatory threshold
* LTHR — lactate-threshold heart rate
* FTP — functional threshold power
* threshold running pace
* critical swim speed / threshold swim pace

Threshold testing can be performed through laboratory testing or validated field protocols.

---

# 4. Lactate Physiology

Blood lactate is not simply a waste product.

Lactate is continuously:

1. produced,
2. transported,
3. oxidized,
4. recycled.

A simplified model is:

$$
\text{Net Lactate Accumulation}
=
\text{Lactate Production}
-
\text{Lactate Clearance}
$$

At low intensity, production and clearance remain relatively balanced.

As intensity increases, lactate concentration begins to rise systematically.

Therefore:

> **Universal lactate values should not automatically be treated as universal physiological thresholds.**

The testing protocol and threshold definition matter.

---

# 6. Heart Rate

Heart rate is a powerful internal-load measurement.

It is particularly useful for controlling:

* long aerobic sessions
* recovery sessions
* endurance cycling
* endurance running

However, heart rate is influenced by:

* temperature
* dehydration
* altitude
* caffeine
* illness
* stress
* sleep
* cardiac drift
* accumulated fatigue

Therefore:

> **Power and pace describe external output; heart rate describes part of the internal physiological response.**

---

# 7. Heart-Rate Zones

A threshold-heart-rate model can be used as a starting point:

| Zone | Approx. % LTHR | Description       |
| ---- | -------------: | ----------------- |
| Z1   |           <85% | Recovery          |
| Z2   |         85–89% | Aerobic endurance |
| Z3   |         90–94% | Tempo             |
| Z4   |        95–100% | Threshold         |
| Z5   |          >100% | High intensity    |

These values should be individualized.

Cycling and running threshold heart rates should generally be treated separately.

---

# 8. Cycling Power

Power provides an excellent external-load measurement.

A fundamental metric is:

$$
IF = \frac{NP}{FTP}
$$

where:

* \(IF\) = Intensity Factor
* \(NP\) = Normalized Power
* \(FTP\) = Functional Threshold Power

Example:

$$
FTP = 300W
$$

$$
NP = 210W
$$

Therefore:

$$
IF = \frac{210}{300}=0.70
$$

---

# 9. Training Stress Score

For cycling:

$$
TSS =
\frac{t \times NP \times IF}
{FTP \times 3600}
\times100
$$

Because:

$$
IF = \frac{NP}{FTP}
$$

this can be simplified to:

$$
TSS =
\frac{t}{3600}
\times IF^2
\times100
$$

where:

* \(t\) = duration in seconds
* \(NP\) = normalized power
* \(FTP\) = functional threshold power
* \(IF\) = intensity factor

---

# 10. Why Intensity Is Expensive

Consider two 60-minute cycling sessions.

### Session A

$$
IF=0.65
$$

$$
TSS=1\times0.65^2\times100
$$

$$
TSS=42.25
$$

### Session B

$$
IF=0.90
$$

$$
TSS=1\times0.90^2\times100
$$

$$
TSS=81
$$

The duration is identical, but the estimated stress is dramatically different.

This illustrates:

> **Intensity has a nonlinear effect on training stress.**

---

# 11. TSS Limitations

TSS is a model, not a direct physiological measurement.

TSS does not fully measure:

* muscle damage
* tendon stress
* glycogen depletion
* psychological stress
* sleep debt
* immune stress
* injury risk
* connective-tissue load

Two workouts with identical TSS can therefore require different recovery times.

For example:

```text
100 TSS easy cycling
```

and

```text
100 TSS high-intensity intervals
```

may have very different recovery requirements.

---

# 12. CTL — Chronic Training Load

CTL represents long-term training-load trends.

A commonly used exponential model is:

$$
CTL_t =
CTL_{t-1}e^{-1/42}
+
TSS_t(1-e^{-1/42})
$$

The conventional time constant is approximately **42 days**.

CTL therefore responds slowly to changes in training.

---

# 13. ATL — Acute Training Load

ATL represents short-term training stress.

A commonly used model is:

$$
ATL_t =
ATL_{t-1}e^{-1/7}
+
TSS_t(1-e^{-1/7})
$$

The conventional time constant is approximately **7 days**.

Therefore:

* CTL changes slowly
* ATL changes quickly

A hard training week can dramatically increase ATL while only gradually increasing CTL.

---

# 14. TSB — Training Stress Balance

The simplified relationship is:

$$
TSB = CTL - ATL
$$

Interpretation:

|           TSB | General interpretation              |
| ------------: | ----------------------------------- |
|      Positive | Relatively fresh                    |
|   Around zero | Balanced                            |
|      Negative | Recent training stress is high      |
| Very negative | High accumulated short-term fatigue |

However:

> **TSB is not an overtraining diagnostic.**

A negative TSB can be completely normal during a productive training block.

---

# 15. Fitness and Fatigue

A useful conceptual model is:

$$
Fitness \approx CTL
$$

$$
Fatigue \approx ATL
$$

$$
Freshness \approx TSB
$$

Therefore:

$$
TSB = CTL - ATL
$$

The objective is not to maximize CTL indefinitely.

The objective is to maximize:

$$
\text{Adaptation}
$$

while maintaining:

$$
\text{Recovery Capacity}
$$

---

# 16. CTL Is Not a Goal by Itself

A rising CTL means training stress is increasing.

It does **not** automatically mean:

* fitness is improving
* health is improving
* performance will improve
* the athlete is recovering adequately

An athlete can produce a rising CTL while simultaneously experiencing:

* declining performance
* poor sleep
* reduced motivation
* persistent soreness
* illness
* injury

Therefore:

> **CTL should be used as a context metric rather than a target to maximize.**

---

# 17. Training Ramp Rate

A simple training-load progression metric is:

$$
Ramp\ Rate =
CTL_{current}-CTL_{previous}
$$

There is no universal safe ramp rate.

Tolerance depends on:

* training age
* previous volume
* injury history
* age
* sleep
* nutrition
* life stress
* running volume

A rapidly rising CTL combined with worsening recovery indicators should trigger caution.

---

# 18. Multi-Sport Training Load

Triathlon requires a combined model:

$$
Total\ Training\ Load =
Swim + Bike + Run + Strength
$$

However, these loads are not physiologically identical.

For example:

$$
100\ TSS_{bike}
\neq
100\ TSS_{run}
$$

from the perspective of mechanical stress.

Running has significantly greater:

* eccentric loading
* tendon loading
* bone loading
* impact
* connective-tissue stress

---

# 19. Running Load Must Be Managed Separately

An athlete may have sufficient cardiovascular fitness to tolerate more running.

The musculoskeletal system may not.

Therefore:

> **Running volume should not be increased simply because total TSS appears manageable.**

Cardiovascular adaptation can occur faster than connective-tissue adaptation.

---

# 20. Swimming Load

Swimming produces relatively little impact but can create significant:

* muscular fatigue
* shoulder stress
* upper-body fatigue
* technical deterioration

Particular attention should be paid to:

* paddle volume
* pull volume
* sprint volume
* shoulder soreness
* technique degradation

Swimming should therefore remain part of the total recovery model.

---

# 21. 80/20 by Sport

The intensity distribution does not have to be identical for all three disciplines.

Example:

| Sport | Possible distribution |
| ----- | --------------------- |
| Swim  | 75–85% easy           |
| Bike  | 75–85% easy           |
| Run   | 80–90% easy           |

Running may require a higher proportion of easy work because of its mechanical cost.

---

# 22. Aerobic Decoupling

Aerobic decoupling describes the deterioration of the relationship between external output and cardiovascular response during prolonged exercise.

For cycling:

$$
Pw:HR =
\frac{Power}{Heart\ Rate}
$$

For example:

### First half

$$
Power=200W
$$

$$
HR=140
$$

### Second half

$$
Power=195W
$$

$$
HR=150
$$

The athlete produces less power while heart rate increases.

Possible causes include:

* dehydration
* heat
* fatigue
* insufficient aerobic durability
* insufficient fueling

---

# 23. Decoupling Formula

A simplified calculation is:

$$
Decoupling =
\frac{EF_{first}-EF_{second}}
{EF_{second}}
\times100
$$

where:

$$
EF = \frac{External\ Output}{Heart\ Rate}
$$

For cycling:

$$
EF = \frac{NP}{Average\ HR}
$$

For running:

$$
EF = \frac{Normalized\ Pace}{Average\ HR}
$$

The comparison is most useful when environmental conditions are similar.

---

# 24. RPE

Rate of Perceived Exertion provides an essential internal measurement.

| RPE | Description      |
| --: | ---------------- |
|   1 | Rest             |
|   2 | Very easy        |
|   3 | Easy             |
|   4 | Moderate         |
|   5 | Comfortably hard |
|   6 | Hard             |
|   7 | Very hard        |
|   8 | Severe           |
|   9 | Near maximal     |
|  10 | Maximal          |

RPE becomes especially valuable when:

* power meter data are questionable
* GPS pace is unreliable
* heat affects HR
* fatigue changes HR response
* terrain changes external output

---

# 25. Internal vs External Load

External load:

* watts
* pace
* distance
* duration
* elevation

Internal load:

* heart rate
* RPE
* lactate
* HRV
* respiratory response

A useful concept is:

$$
\frac{Internal\ Load}{External\ Load}
$$

If the athlete suddenly needs substantially greater internal effort to produce the same external output, fatigue or environmental stress may be present.

---

# 26. HRV

Heart-rate variability can provide useful information about recovery status.

Do not rely on one measurement.

Instead, compare:

$$
HRV_{today}
$$

with:

$$
HRV_{baseline}
$$

and preferably a rolling baseline.

A stronger warning pattern is:

$$
HRV\downarrow
+
RHR\uparrow
+
RPE\uparrow
+
Performance\downarrow
$$

rather than HRV reduction alone.

---

# 27. Resting Heart Rate

Track morning resting heart rate under consistent conditions.

For example:

$$
RHR_{today}
-
RHR_{baseline}
$$

A persistent increase may indicate:

* fatigue
* poor recovery
* dehydration
* illness
* stress
* heat exposure
* insufficient sleep

An isolated increase is not necessarily significant.

---

# 28. Recovery Dashboard

Track:

| Variable    | Normal                  | Warning              |
| ----------- | ----------------------- | -------------------- |
| Sleep       | Normal duration/quality | Reduced              |
| RHR         | Baseline                | Elevated             |
| HRV         | Baseline                | Persistent reduction |
| RPE         | Normal                  | Elevated             |
| Mood        | Good                    | Irritable/flat       |
| Motivation  | Normal                  | Reduced              |
| Soreness    | Low                     | Persistent           |
| Performance | Normal                  | Declining            |

The combination of multiple warning signs is more useful than any single metric.

# 29. Traffic-Light Training System

## Green

Proceed with planned training.

## Yellow

Modify training:

* reduce duration
* reduce intensity
* remove intervals
* convert workout to Z1/Z2
* remove brick
* add recovery

## Red

Avoid forcing training.

Prioritize:

* recovery
* sleep
* hydration
* nutrition
* medical assessment when appropriate

# 30. Productive Overreaching

Short-term fatigue can be intentionally introduced.

Example:

```text
Training Load
     ↑
     │       █ █ █
     │       █ █ █
     │     █ █ █ █
     │   █ █ █ █ █
     │
     └─────────────────
          Recovery
              ↓
         Performance
             ↑
```

A productive block may look like:

$$
3\ weeks\ loading
\rightarrow
1\ week\ recovery
\rightarrow
performance\ rebound
$$

---

# 31. Non-Functional Overreaching

A warning occurs when fatigue becomes excessive and performance does not rebound as expected.

Potential signs:

* persistent performance decline
* prolonged fatigue
* poor sleep
* reduced motivation
* repeated failed workouts
* increased illness
* persistent soreness

---

# 32. Overtraining Syndrome

Overtraining syndrome is considerably more serious than ordinary training fatigue.

It should not be diagnosed from:

* CTL
* ATL
* TSB
* HRV
* resting HR

alone.

Persistent unexplained performance decline or significant symptoms require appropriate professional assessment.

---

# 33. Energy Availability

Training load cannot be separated from nutrition.

Insufficient energy availability may impair:

* recovery
* immune function
* endocrine function
* bone health
* mood
* performance

Therefore:

$$
Training\ Stress
+
Energy\ Deficit
$$

can produce substantially more physiological stress than training stress alone.

---

# 34. Carbohydrate Availability

High-intensity training relies heavily on carbohydrate.

A useful strategy is:

> **Fuel the sessions that matter most.**

Hard sessions should generally be performed with adequate carbohydrate availability.

Long sessions require individualized carbohydrate and fluid strategies based on:

* duration
* intensity
* body size
* temperature
* sweat rate
* gastrointestinal tolerance

---

# 35. Key Training Sessions

A triathlete should normally identify only a small number of truly important sessions.

Example:

### Tuesday

Bike intervals

### Thursday

Swim threshold / VO₂

### Saturday

Long race-specific bike + transition run

The rest of the week supports these sessions.

---

A practical structure is:


---

# 38. Two-to-Three Hard Sessions per Week

A practical starting point for many age-group athletes is:

$$
2–3
$$

key intensity sessions per week.

More is not necessarily better.

An athlete with limited recovery capacity may perform better with only two.

Elite athletes with high training tolerance may tolerate more.

---

# 39. Brick Training

Brick workouts are valuable because they develop:

* transition skill
* running under cycling fatigue
* race-specific pacing
* neuromuscular adaptation

But they can also be expensive.

A useful default is:

$$
Long\ Bike_{easy/moderate}
+
Short\ Run_{easy/race-specific}
$$

rather than making every brick a maximal session.

# 43. Training Stress Distribution

An athlete can have:

```text
80% easy / 20% hard
```

and still accumulate excessive stress.

Conversely:

```text
90% easy / 10% hard
```

can sometimes be insufficiently stimulating.

Therefore:

$$
Intensity\ Distribution
\neq
Total\ Training\ Load
$$

Both must be monitored.

# 45. Recovery Weeks

A recovery week commonly includes:

* reduced total volume
* substantially reduced TSS
* reduced running impact
* continued technical work
* small amounts of intensity
* increased sleep
* increased fueling and recovery

A recovery week is not lost training.

It is when adaptation can become visible.

---

# 46. Maintaining Intensity During a Taper

A taper generally reduces volume while retaining selected intensity.

Conceptually:

$$
ATL\downarrow
$$

while:

$$
CTL\downarrow\ slowly
$$

and:

$$
TSB\uparrow
$$

The goal is:

$$
Fatigue\downarrow
$$

while:

$$
Fitness\approx maintained
$$

---

# 47. Race-Day TSB

There is no universal ideal TSB.

Some athletes perform well with slightly negative TSB.

Others perform better with positive TSB.

The correct value should be determined from the athlete's own history.

Use:

* previous races
* successful key workouts
* subjective freshness
* HR/RPE response

to identify the individual's optimal race-readiness range.

---

# 48. Long-Distance Pacing

For 70.3 and Ironman:

> **The objective is not to maximize bike performance in isolation.**

The objective is to maximize:

$$
Bike + Run
$$

A bike that is slightly slower but leaves the athlete capable of producing a strong run may produce a faster total race.

---

# 56. Practical Anti-Overtraining Rules

## Rule 1

Keep approximately 80% of training genuinely easy.

## Rule 2

Limit hard sessions to those with a clear purpose.

## Rule 3

Do not increase volume and intensity aggressively at the same time.

## Rule 4

Protect key workouts.

## Rule 5

Monitor CTL and ATL together.

## Rule 6

Use TSB as context, not diagnosis.

## Rule 7

Treat running load separately from metabolic load.

## Rule 8

Fuel hard and long sessions.

## Rule 9

Prioritize sleep.

## Rule 10

Respond to trends rather than isolated data points.

# 57. The Core Mathematical Model

A useful conceptual model is:

$$
Adaptation =
Training\ Stimulus
\times
Recovery
$$

If recovery approaches zero:

$$
Adaptation\rightarrow0
$$

Therefore:

$$
More\ Training
\neq
More\ Fitness
$$

Instead:

$$
Optimal\ Training
=
Maximum\ Sustainable\ Stimulus
$$

```text
            ADAPTATION
                 │
                 ▼
           PERFORMANCE
                 │
                 ▼
          DATA ANALYSIS
                 │
                 ▼
        ADJUST NEXT LOAD
                 │
                 └───────────────► TRAINING
```

# 60. Final Framework

The purpose of training-load management is not to produce the highest possible:

$$
CTL
$$

or:

$$
TSS
$$

The objective is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Maximum Sustainable Performance}
}
$$

subject to:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Recovery Capacity}
\geq
\text{Training Demand}
}
$$

The best training program is therefore not the hardest program.

It is the program that the athlete can:

1. execute consistently,
2. recover from,
3. adapt to,
4. repeat,
5. progressively overload,
6. and ultimately convert into race performance.

# 61. Executive Summary

For triathlon, an effective load-management strategy combines **80/20 intensity distribution** with quantitative and qualitative monitoring.

The 80/20 framework separates easy aerobic volume from purposeful intensity.

TSS estimates training stress.

CTL describes long-term training-load trends.

ATL describes short-term training stress.

TSB describes the relationship between chronic and acute load.

Lactate and ventilatory thresholds provide physiological anchors.

Heart rate provides internal-load information.

Power and pace measure external performance.

RPE provides immediate subjective feedback.

HRV, resting heart rate, sleep and mood provide recovery context.

The key principle is:

$$
\boxed{
Stimulus
\rightarrow
Recovery
\rightarrow
Adaptation
\rightarrow
Performance
}
$$

not:

$$
\boxed{
Training
\rightarrow
Fatigue
\rightarrow
More\ Training
\rightarrow
Breakdown
}
$$

Ultimately:

> **The best triathlon training load is the largest load an athlete can repeatedly absorb, recover from and convert into improved performance.**
